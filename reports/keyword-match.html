Does having the query keyword in your business name improve local rank?

6,439 business-by-scan observations · 121 cached Local Falcon geo-grid reports · 29 markets · 3,424 distinct businesses · generated 2026-09-02 · entirely offline from cached scans

Verdict — two different answers, and the folklore gets the wrong one. Having the query's head noun in the business name shows no association with position within a ranked field. In the strongest available design — the same business, in the same market, scanned the same day, compared across the query its name matches and the query it does not, with both the business and the keyword held fixed by fixed effects — the estimate is 0.77 percentile points (95% CI -0.81 to 2.34, p = 0.34, 702 businesses). That is a clean null, and it holds after removing the agency's own renamed client locations, after restricting to large fields, and after restricting to genuine cannabis retailers.

Where the name does associate with something is one step earlier: whether the business appears in the field at all. Across the comparable-field-size contrast (cannabis vs dispensary queries, where both directions of the test are available and a field-size artefact would cancel), matching the query noun associates with a 10.3-percentage-point higher chance of surfacing in that query's results (95% CI 6.0 to 14.0, p = 1.7×10−6). But that effect is almost entirely concentrated in businesses Google does not categorise as cannabis retailers — vape shops, smoke shops, gift shops, wellness stores — where the estimate is +68 points. Among actual cannabis-category dispensaries under the same strict contrast it falls to +3.7 points (p = 0.064) — indistinguishable from nothing.

What that means for a dispensary. Renaming a licensed dispensary to insert "Cannabis" or "Dispensary" is not the cheap rank lever it appears to be — for a business Google already classifies as a cannabis store, the keyword in the name buys essentially nothing, either in field entry or in position. The name appears to act as a substitute for category relevance, not an amplifier of it. The seed observation that started this — High Society New Buffalo ranking 1st for "weed" and 12th for "cannabis" — is reproduced faithfully below, but it is explained by which competitors Google admits into each field, not by the name earning a better position inside one.

This is observational geo-grid data. Everything below is stated as association. Nothing here establishes causation, and a name change was never randomised or observed changing.

How the analysis sets were cut

  • SET A (primary) — the "weed near me" / "cannabis near me" / "dispensary near me" trio, one report per (market, keyword) = the most recent scan. 2-mile radius, 11x11 grid. 17 repeat scans of the same market and keyword were dropped so a single heavily-rescanned market cannot dominate. Field sizes run 3–135 (median 78).
  • SET B — New Buffalo MI, all 7 queries scanned on 2026-08-29 at 2-mile radius. 212 rows over 7 queries, which is the cleanest possible "one business, many queries, one day" case study.
  • SET C — New Buffalo MI, city-modified queries on 100-mile grids (Mar/Apr 2026). Used only for the city-token question.
  • Excluded — the keyword mi: a 100-mile grid on a bare state abbreviation; 1129 places, not a real local query. Present in observations.csv (junk_keyword=1), excluded from every test.

How the two variables are defined

  • Outcome (rank) — pct = 100 * (#field beaten + 0.5*#tied) / (field_n - 1), computed within each report from SoLV. 100 = beats the whole field. A percentile, not a raw position, because field sizes differ by a factor of forty. It is tie-aware: SoLV is heavily tied at 0.00 (a third of a typical field never surfaces anywhere on the grid), so mid-ranks are mandatory.
  • Exposure (name match) — name_has_head = the business name contains every vertical noun present in the query (weed / cannabis / dispensar-). Substring match on a lowercased, punctuation-stripped name.

Percentile rank for businesses whose name contains the query's head noun versus those whose name does not, computed separately within each keyword and never pooled, because field size and competitive intensity differ wildly between these queries. 3. Controls — does it survive review count, rating, proximity and category?

The obvious confound is that a business named "X Cannabis" may simply be a bigger or better-placed business. Three independent control strategies are applied, all within keyword.

  • Stratification — a van Elteren-style stratified rank-sum test, with strata formed from review quartile crossed with cannabis-category status, so like is only ever compared with like.
  • Partial correlation — Spearman partial correlation of name match with percentile, controlling log review count, star rating, distance from the grid centre (computed by haversine from each business's coordinates — proximity is the dominant term in local ranking and was not in the original brief) and cannabis-category status.
  • Regression — OLS with report fixed effects (so every comparison is inside a single scan) and cluster-robust standard errors by report.

Every control tells the same story: "cannabis near me" and "weed near me" are null under every specification, and "dispensary near me" retains a small positive residual (about 3.8 percentile points with report fixed effects, p = 0.038) that is far smaller than the raw 8.0-point gap.

One control was deliberately not applied. The brief listed found_in as a control. found_in is the number of grid points where a business appeared, and SoLV — the outcome — is computed from those same appearances. It is a component of the outcome, not a covariate; conditioning on it would be conditioning on a mediator and would bias the estimate toward zero by construction. It is reported in the JSON as partial_spearman_with_mediator and flagged there, but it is not used for any conclusion.

4. The within-business test — the strongest evidence available

This holds the business completely constant. A business that appears in several of its market's keyword scans has the same location, the same reviews, the same rating, the same photos and the same profile in every one of them. The only thing that changes is the query. If the name mattered, its percentile should be higher on the query its name matches.

Read the first two rows together. Cannabis-named businesses do 3.13 points worse on "cannabis near me" than on "dispensary near me", and dispensary-named businesses do 3.09 points better on "dispensary near me" than on "cannabis near me". Those are the same statement. Both groups sit higher on the "dispensary near me" scan regardless of what they are called — that is a keyword main effect, not a name effect, and a raw paired difference cannot tell the two apart.

The estimator that can is a two-way fixed-effects model: percentile regressed on name match, absorbing both a business×market fixed effect (which removes everything permanent about the business) and a keyword fixed effect (which removes how generous each query is overall), with standard errors clustered by business×market.

Every specification straddles zero. Grey = not significant at p < 0.05.

Once the keyword main effect is removed, the name is worth 0.77 percentile points (95% CI -0.81 to 2.34, p = 0.34). The confidence interval excludes anything larger than about 2.3 percentile points — on a typical 78-business field that is under one position. The hypothesis, as stated, does not survive.

5. Where the name does associate with something: getting into the field at all

The analysis above asks about position within a field. But a business only has a position if Google returned it. Local Falcon's places list is exactly the set of businesses that surfaced in the top 20 at one or more of the 121 grid points — so being in the field is itself a ranking outcome, and a much more consequential one than moving from 14th to 11th.

The composition of the three fields is startling on its own.

Share of each query's returned field by what the business is called. "weed near me" returns a small field that is overwhelmingly weed-named; "dispensary near me" returns a field four times larger, half of which carries no vertical noun at all.

The artefact check that matters

The headline entry number is inflated by field size: "weed near me" returns a median field of 16 against 71 for "dispensary near me", so almost nobody is in the weed field. The clean test uses only cannabis vs dispensary, whose fields are comparable, and where both directions of the contrast are available. A field-size artefact would push both directions the same way; a real name effect pushes them in opposite directions.

Both directions favour the business's own token, which a field-size artefact cannot produce, so this one is real: +10.3 percentage points (95% CI 6.0 to 14.0). McNemar's test is used here because the outcome is paired and binary. Across markets the direction is consistent — 19 of 27 markets positive, 126 discordant business-query pairs favouring the name match against 41 against — so it is not one or two markets driving it.

But it belongs almost entirely to businesses that are not dispensaries

Splitting that same contrast by whether Google gives the business a cannabis category separates the mechanism cleanly. For a business with no cannabis category — a vape shop, smoke shop, herb shop, gift shop — the name is close to the only relevance signal it has, and the association is enormous. For a licensed dispensary that Google already classifies as a cannabis store, it collapses to nothing.

10. What is not established

  • Causation. This is observational geo-grid data. No business was observed changing its name, so nothing here can say what would happen if one did. Every statement above is an association.
  • The name versus everything correlated with the name. A business called "X Cannabis" almost certainly also says "cannabis" in its GBP description, services, website title and review text. The business×market fixed effect removes everything permanent about the business, but it cannot separate the name from other on-profile signals that vary with the same query. What is measured is "name token matches query", not "the name field specifically causes this".
  • Field entry is measured through Local Falcon's top-20 capture, not through Google directly. A business absent from the places list did not reach the top 20 at any of the 121 grid points; it is not proof Google holds no record of it for that query.
  • "weed near me" is thin. Only 85 of 475 rows are non-matching names, so that keyword's within-field test has little power. Its null should be read as "no evidence", not "evidence of no effect".
  • The city-token question is under-powered. SET C has one market and one scan per keyword, so the "does the city in the name help on city queries" results in the JSON (per_keyword.set_c_city_token) are reported but not relied on.
  • Multiplicity. BH-FDR is applied within each family of keyword tests. It is not applied across the whole report; the headline conclusions rest on the pre-specified within-business design, not on the smallest p-value found.
